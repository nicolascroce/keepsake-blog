There is a specific kind of small failure that most people recognise instantly. Someone you have met twice asks how the thing you told them about went — by name, with the details right. You reciprocate with a generic question, because you cannot remember whether they moved cities, what their company actually does, or whether the child they mentioned is a son or a daughter.

The usual diagnosis is "I'm bad with names." <mark>The accurate diagnosis is that you were relying on recall for a job that only capture can do.</mark>

## Recall and capture are not the same tool

Recall is what your brain does with information it has already encoded. Capture is the act of encoding it somewhere durable in the first place.

Human memory encodes reliably under three conditions: repetition, emotional weight, or attachment to an existing structure. Details about other people meet none of them.

You hear them **once**. They carry **no emotional charge** — a stranger's daughter's name is not going to move you. And they have **nothing to attach to**: if you know a hundred people professionally, their children's names are an unstructured list of a hundred items.

Then there is volume. A hundred to two hundred active relationships, roughly ten retrievable facts each, and those facts change: people move, switch jobs, have another kid, finish the project they were describing. You are not being asked to remember a thousand things. You are being asked to remember a thousand things *and keep them current*.

No one does this by recall. Everyone who appears to be doing it is capturing.

## The timing problem

The reason this feels harder than it is comes down to timing.

The information arrives at the exact moment it is worthless — mid-conversation, when you have no use for the name of the town they are moving to. It becomes valuable eight months later, when it is gone.

Every working system solves this the same way: it moves a small amount of effort from the moment the information is *needed*, where no amount of effort helps, to the moment it is *available*, where it costs about two minutes.

## What the two minutes look like

After a conversation that mattered, write down what you learned, attached to the person.

Not minutes. Not a summary. Something closer to this:

> Coffee, 14 March. Daughter Lea starting secondary school. Moving to Nantes in September, still looking for a school. Working on an internal tooling rebuild — mentioned the budget is tight.

Four things make this outperform any attempt to memorise the same content.

**It is dated.** Eight months on, you know "starting secondary school" was true in March, so she is a year further along now. Facts about people expire; an undated note quietly becomes a wrong note.

**It is attached to a person, not to a day.** You will never search by date. You will always search by person. A note filed chronologically is stored but not retrievable, which in practice is the same as lost.

**It records context, not just facts.** "Budget is tight" is not trivia. It is the reason you will not lead with your most expensive option next time.

**It is short enough to actually happen.** Any system that takes longer than the conversation itself will be abandoned within a month.

## Why the obvious tools fail

Most people have already tried something. The failures are consistent enough to be predictable.

**A notebook.** Notes exist, but retrieval is linear. Answering "what do I know about this person" means re-reading months of pages, so you stop asking.

**A spreadsheet.** Fine while it is one row per person and three columns. It breaks the moment history accumulates — a conversation does not fit in a cell, and a cell does not preserve three conversations in sequence.

**A sales CRM.** Built around pipelines: stages, deal values, close probabilities. None of that describes someone you like and see twice a year. The tool imposes a commercial intent that is not there, and you feel it every time you open it.

**An over-designed personal system.** Custom fields, relationship tiers, automated nudges, scoring. It survives three weeks. Anything that turns relationship upkeep into a chore competes with real work and loses.

The pattern: capture has to cost less than the value of one retrieved detail. That budget is small — two minutes — and most tools blow through it before you have written anything.

## What changes after six months

Nothing visible happens in week one. The payoff arrives the first time a note surfaces at the right moment.

You skim three lines before a call and ask how the school search went. You come across an article about precisely the rebuild they described, and forward it without manufacturing a reason to get in touch. You notice it has been eleven months since you last spoke — and you say so, instead of pretending it was recent.

None of those moves require effort when it counts. They required two minutes, eight months earlier.

This is what a reputation for "remembering everything" is actually made of. It does not reward an unusual memory. It rewards a modest system, kept up consistently, that no one else ever sees.

## Where to start

Do not try to backfill. Systems like this are never populated retroactively — the information is already gone, and the attempt is discouraging enough to end the habit before it starts.

Start at the next conversation that matters. Three lines, dated, attached to the person. Then the next one. After three months you will have thirty or so, and the first time one surfaces at exactly the right moment, the question of whether it was worth it stops being a question.
